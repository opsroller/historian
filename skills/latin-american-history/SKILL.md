# Latin American history

The region's past is long, plural and recorded unevenly. Indigenous
peoples kept records in painted books, carved inscriptions, knotted
cords and memory; Europeans and Africans arrived from 1492; most
surviving written records after that were made under colonial rule.
The method is the same as anywhere (`historical-research`,
`source-criticism`); what changes is where to look and how to read.

## Periods and peoples (a map, not a canon)

| Period | Notes for research |
|---|---|
| Before contact | Mesoamerica (among others Olmec, Maya, Zapotec, Mixtec, Teotihuacan, Toltec, Tarascan/P'urhépecha, and the Nahua peoples including the Mexica of Tenochtitlan); the Andes (among others Chavín, Moche, Nazca, Tiwanaku, Wari, Chimú, Inca); the Caribbean (Taíno and others); Amazonia and the southern cone. Evidence is mostly archaeological, epigraphic and from post-contact accounts |
| Contact and conquest | From 1492 in the Caribbean, 1519-21 in central Mexico, 1532 onward in the Andes, and later elsewhere. Indigenous allies did much of the fighting; disease shaped every outcome |
| Colonial | Viceroyalties of New Spain and Peru (later New Granada and Río de la Plata), Portuguese Brazil, other European colonies in the Caribbean and Guianas; Indigenous towns, missions, mining, haciendas, the Atlantic slave trade and African and Afro-descendant communities |
| Independence | Haiti (1804), Spanish America (1810s-1820s), Brazil (1822); Cuba and Puerto Rico later |
| Modern nations | Nation-building, liberal reform, revolutions, migration, Indigenous and Afro-descendant movements |

Ask always: whose period is this? Dates of conquest are not dates when
Indigenous societies ended.

## Source types

- **Indigenous painted books and documents**, before and after contact:
  ritual and calendrical books, histories, genealogies, maps, tribute
  records, land claims (codices, lienzos, mapas).
- **Inscriptions and monuments**, notably Maya hieroglyphic texts,
  readable through modern epigraphy.
- **Khipu** (Andean knotted cords) and the colonial transcriptions that
  survive of some of them.
- **Alphabetic texts in Indigenous languages** written after contact:
  annals, testaments, petitions, town records, sacred and historical
  narratives.
- **Colonial chronicles and ethnographies** by friars, soldiers,
  officials and Indigenous and mestizo authors, often made with
  Indigenous informants and painters.
- **Administrative and legal records:** surveys of towns, censuses,
  tribute and land records, lawsuits, Inquisition trials, notarial and
  parish records.
- **Archaeology:** excavation reports, site records, museum collections.

## Where to look

- National archives and libraries of each country (for Mexico the
  Archivo General de la Nación and the Biblioteca Nacional de
  Antropología e Historia), and Spain's Archivo General de Indias in
  Seville, much of it searchable through the PARES portal.
- National institutes of anthropology and history and their museums
  (for Mexico, INAH).
- Research libraries with major Latin American collections in the
  Americas and Europe; many have digitized their codices and early
  books.
- Digital editions of major sources, for example the Getty Research
  Institute's Digital Florentine Codex (Nahuatl and Spanish text,
  translations and searchable images) and the digital Codex Mendoza.
- The Library of Congress's Handbook of Latin American Studies, an
  annotated bibliography of scholarship, to find the literature.
- Journals such as the Hispanic American Historical Review, and the
  work of the Conference on Latin American History and the Latin
  American Studies Association.

Check every holding and identifier against the institution's own
catalogue before citing it.

## Languages and names

- Read Spanish and Portuguese sources in the original where possible;
  for Indigenous-language sources use scholarly editions with the
  original text and name the translator.
- Name peoples as they named themselves or as current scholarship and
  their descendants do, and note the common name: "Mexica" for the
  people of Tenochtitlan, with "Aztec" as the popular term; "Nahua" for
  Nahuatl speakers generally; "Inca" for the state and its rulers, with
  "Tawantinsuyu" its own name.
- Keep original spellings of names and terms with a modern form
  alongside, and use the same form everywhere in the product.
- Avoid terms that carry the conqueror's view as neutral ("discovery",
  "New World" without quotation marks, "pre-Columbian" where a period
  name does better) unless quoting.

## Debates to know

- **Whose account of conquest:** older narratives built on conquerors'
  letters stressed a few Europeans; recent scholarship ("new conquest
  history") stresses Indigenous allies, Indigenous-language sources,
  disease and long negotiation.
- **Colonial sources about Indigenous practice:** friars recorded
  religion and society to convert, and informants answered under
  colonial power; such sources are indispensable and partial.
- **Sacrifice, violence and spectacle:** separate archaeological
  evidence from colonial rhetoric, and do not make violence the
  defining image of a people.
- **Population and demographic collapse:** estimates vary widely;
  cite the range and the method.
- **Mestizaje and identity:** national narratives of mixture have their
  own politics; Indigenous and Afro-descendant histories are not a
  footnote to them.

## Living communities

Millions of people today descend from and continue the cultures this
history covers, speaking Nahuatl, Maya languages, Quechua, Aymara,
Guaraní and many more. Their traditions are living culture, not
props. Apply `heritage-ethics` to every depiction.

## Quality bar

- Periods and peoples are named precisely (which people, which place,
  which decades), never "ancient Mexico" or "the Indians".
- Colonial sources are read as colonial sources, with Indigenous and
  community sources sought beside them.
- Holdings and identifiers are checked against the holder's catalogue.

## Documentation owed

A timeline and a glossary or naming guide for the product's period and
place (**reference**); an **explanation** of the debates a design
choice depends on.
