# Source criticism

A source is evidence of what its maker could know, chose to record and
was able to say. Whether a source is primary or secondary depends on
the question asked of it: a sixteenth-century chronicle is primary for
what its author believed and secondary for events decades before.

## The questions to ask of every source

| Ask | Why |
|---|---|
| **Who made it?** Author, informants, scribes, painters, translators, editors | Each hand shapes it; many sources are collaborative |
| **When and where?** Date of making, not of the events | Distance in time and place lowers what the maker could know |
| **For whom and why?** Audience, commission, purpose (legal claim, petition, propaganda, instruction, devotion, record) | Purpose decides what is stressed, softened or left out |
| **How could the maker know?** Witness, informant, earlier document, hearsay | Separate what was seen from what was heard |
| **What kind of thing is it?** Genre and its conventions | A tribute list, a court case, a conversion manual and a painted history each lie and tell truth differently |
| **How did it reach us?** Original, copy, later redaction, edition, translation; where it is held and how it got there | Copies drift; editions select; translations interpret |
| **What does it not say?** Who is absent or spoken for | The record over-represents the literate, the powerful and the winners |

## Provenance and editions

- Name the holding institution and shelf mark, folio or object number.
- Prefer the original or a facsimile; then a scholarly edition that
  shows the original language and says what it changed; then a
  translation, named as such with its translator and date.
- Note when a translation is itself old or contested, and check key
  terms against the original language where you can.
- Treat a "copy of a lost original" as evidence of the copy's time.

## Bias of the record

Many records were made by outsiders, officials or conquerors about
people who could not answer in that record. Use them, but:

- Ask who the record served, and what its makers needed to believe.
- Read against the grain: details recorded in passing, names, prices,
  complaints and testimony can show what the record's purpose hides.
- Seek the community's own sources (their writings, images, maps,
  annals, oral tradition recorded with consent) and recent scholarship
  that reads them.
- Do not treat absence from the record as absence from history.

## Corroboration

- For any claim the product builds on, find an independent second
  source: one that did not copy from the first or share its informant.
- When sources agree only because one copied the other, they count as
  one.
- When they conflict, record both, why they might differ, and which
  current scholarship favours and why.

## Grades

| Grade | Use when |
|---|---|
| Attested | A reliable source with access says it directly, ideally corroborated |
| Inferred | It follows from evidence by an argument you state |
| Contested | Sources or scholars disagree; name both sides |
| Unknown | No evidence found; say where you looked and what would settle it |

## Fakes and errors

Check that an object or document is what it claims to be: museum and
catalogue records, excavation context, scholarly authentication. Known
forgeries and misattributions exist for every collecting market; an
object without provenance or excavation context is weak evidence for
how anything looked.

## Quality bar

- Every source used has maker, date, purpose, access and holding noted.
- Translations and editions are named; key terms checked where they
  carry weight.
- Every claim the product builds on is corroborated or marked as resting
  on one source.
- Conflicts are recorded, not hidden.

## Documentation owed

The judgement of each major source in the research brief's source
notes (**reference**). A recurring source with known problems gets its
own reference entry, so the next brief does not judge it again.
